## Overview

David Chang has consistently engaged with questions of Korean-American identity, cultural appropriation, and what it means for an Asian-American chef to cook "American food." These themes run through his restaurants, media work, and public statements.

## HuffPost: "David Chang: People Should Honor The Cultures That Ethnic Food Comes From"

Published coinciding with Ugly Delicious Season 1 (2018):

Chang argued that assimilation kills "the new" and that "a march towards efficiency" ruins the diversity of food.

He noted it was hard growing up to say he liked Korean food — many people grow up embarrassed about their ethnic foods but reclaim and embrace them over time.

Chang described the goal of Ugly Delicious as bringing about dialogue on immigration and assimilation, and what happens when the already-assimilated turn a blind eye to other minority groups.

## The Authenticity Problem

Chang has consistently pushed back against being expected to authenticate or represent Korean food:

"I've never said my food's Korean... I've always said that it's American food."

"Being a first-generation Asian American means existing nowhere—in the US, he is seen as Korean first despite growing up in Virginia, and in Korea he doesn't speak the language and is treated like an American."

He is comfortable with being "something in between" rather than being exclusively American or Korean.

## Salon: The Kimchi Revolution (2014)

Salon's 2014 piece on "How Korean-American chefs are changing food culture" placed Chang at the center of a broader movement:
- Chang and fellow Korean-American chefs (Roy Choi, etc.) were credited with mainstreaming Korean flavors in American restaurants
- The piece noted the paradox: Chang deliberately refused to call his food Korean, yet became the most influential figure in bringing Korean-inspired flavors to American mainstream food culture
- "The Kimchi Revolution" framed this as a generational shift in how Korean-Americans engaged with their culinary heritage

## Taste Cooking: "It's Not Korean, It's Korean With a Hyphen"

This piece explored the hyphenated identity question directly — the experience of being Korean-American (not fully either) and what that means for how one cooks:
- Chang's food is described as operating in the hyphen: drawing from Korean traditions without claiming Korean authenticity
- This hyphenated positioning actually gave him creative freedom: he could remix Korean ingredients and techniques with American, Japanese, French, and other influences without being bound by tradition

## Ugly Delicious as Cultural Commentary

NBCNews: "Netflix's 'Ugly Delicious' succeeds because David Chang understands that food is political."

The show used food as a vehicle to discuss:
- Immigration and who gets credit for cultural contributions
- Racism in how different ethnic cuisines are valued
- Cultural appropriation vs. cultural appreciation
- The politics of "authenticity"

## The Chili Crunch Controversy in Context

The 2024 chili crunch trademark controversy is directly legible through this identity framework. Chang had built his brand partly on Korean-American authenticity and advocacy for AAPI food culture — making the cease-and-desist actions against other AAPI brands particularly damaging to his brand identity. The community's "You are a culinary colonizer" critique cut to the heart of the tension between Chang's stated values and the trademark enforcement action.

## Council of Korean Americans: Inspire Award

Chang received the Council of Korean Americans' Inspire Award — recognition from the Korean-American community for his role in elevating Korean food and culture in American consciousness. The award underscores how central this identity dimension is to his public persona and brand.

## Significance for Hero Study

Chang's engagement with Korean-American identity is not peripheral to his business story — it IS a core competitive advantage. The authenticity and cultural credibility he built through this engagement:
1. Gave Ugly Delicious its sociocultural edge (making it more than a cooking show)
2. Built AAPI community goodwill that made the chili crunch controversy so damaging
3. Differentiated Momofuku Goods products from generic Asian-flavored CPG brands
4. Created a loyal customer base that sees purchasing Momofuku products as supporting AAPI entrepreneurship
