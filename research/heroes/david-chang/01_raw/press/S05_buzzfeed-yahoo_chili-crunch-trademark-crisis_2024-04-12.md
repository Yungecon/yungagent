## Overview

In April 2024, Momofuku triggered a major PR crisis when it sent cease-and-desist letters to at least seven AAPI-owned businesses that used the term "chili crunch" in their condiment names — while Momofuku's trademark application for the term was still pending with the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office.

## The Trademark Strategy

In 2020, Momofuku began selling jars of "chili crunch" — a spicy condiment made with chilis and oil commonly found across many Asian cultures. The product took off exponentially across the American grocery market in 2021.

In March 2024, Momofuku sent seven cease-and-desist letters to companies calling their product "Chili Crunch" or "Chile Crunch." Most of the companies that received the letter were small brands founded by Asian Americans.

## The Backlash

Chang was called everything from a "trademark bully" to a "culinary colonizer" and was even accused of attempting to "whitewash" the condiment aisle.

Michelle Tew, owner of Malaysian food brand Homiah — one of the companies that received the letter — wrote that the letter "felt like a punch in the gut" and expressed sadness that it came from Momofuku specifically: "It's a disappointing turn of events for someone who so singularly represents AAPI in the food & beverage industry."

Several small Asian markets and stores pledged to no longer purchase goods from the brand. A full consumer boycott was called for on social media.

## The Risk Calculation

During the initial controversy, the CEO Marguerite Mariscal reportedly acknowledged the risk, stating "It's a risk we're willing to take" — a quote that further inflamed public opinion.

## The Reversal and Apology

On the April 12, 2024 episode of The Dave Chang Show podcast, Chang and Mariscal announced they were no longer enforcing the trademark.

Chang's apology: "I want to apologize to everyone in the AAPI community who's been hurt or feels like I marginalized them."

## Business Impact and Context

The controversy illuminated a tension at the heart of Momofuku's brand identity: Chang built his reputation and empire on celebrating and popularizing Asian flavors in America — but the trademark action was perceived as commodifying and gatekeeping that very cultural heritage.

The episode was closely watched by the food and beverage industry as a case study in how brand equity built on cultural identity can be rapidly damaged when that identity appears to conflict with aggressive IP strategy.

## Resolution

Momofuku ultimately dropped the trademark enforcement. The episode did not appear to have a lasting financial impact on Momofuku Goods' growth, which continued to accelerate through 2024 ($67M revenue).

## Broader Context: "Chili Crunch" vs. "Chili Crisp"

Lao Gan Ma — a Chinese brand — had been producing a nearly identical condiment (known as "chili crisp") for decades before Momofuku's entry. Critics pointed to this history to underscore the absurdity of Momofuku claiming ownership of the "crunch" variant. NBC News reported: "Lao Gan Ma was in fridges long before Momofuku's chili crunch was in headlines."
