## Overview

David Chang's memoir "Eat a Peach" (published September 2020, Crown publishing) is a raw and honest account of his rise to culinary stardom, his struggles with mental illness, his thoughts on culture and identity, and how he enacted his vision of a new way of eating in America.

Chang introduced the book by stating he did not believe it was a memoir but rather "a textbook on what not to do when starting a business."

## Mental Health Disclosure

"Eat a Peach" contained David Chang's most public and detailed discussion of his bipolar disorder diagnosis. Key disclosures:
- Bipolar disorder diagnosis: Chang revealed the diagnosis publicly
- Affective dysregulation: Chang described intense anger as a symptom
- Depression: Lifelong struggles with depression documented
- Momofuku as therapy: "Momofuku became therapy in addition to the therapy that he began with a psychiatrist, and so much of who he is today, and what Momofuku was, is intricately intertwined with his mental health."

## NPR Interview Themes

NPR's coverage of Chang (Eat a Peach interview and prior interviews) covered:
- Depression and being a dad: Chang's evolution as a father — having children shifted his sense of purpose beyond restaurants
- The burden of authenticity: Chang's discomfort with being expected to represent or authenticate Korean-American food and culture
- How cooking "saved his life"
- His evolution from an angry, driven chef into a more reflective person

## Business Philosophy from the Memoir

The memoir documents Momofuku's inception through the lens of extreme risk-taking and near-failure:
- The first Momofuku Noodle Bar was considered likely to fail by industry observers
- Chang took on personal debt and leveraged family relationships to open
- He describes early years of the restaurants as chaotic, driven by fear and rage as much as passion
- His mental health crises intersected directly with business decisions

## Key Insights on Building Momofuku

1. **Failure as expectation**: Chang built the first restaurant expecting it to fail — this freed him to take risks he would never have taken if he expected success
2. **Korea as negative model**: He studied in Japan partly to escape the pressure of Korean-American identity expectations
3. **Creative self-destruction**: He repeatedly describes himself as someone who tears down what he builds — a recurring theme with restaurant closures in later years
4. **Partnership as survival**: His collaborations (Peter Meehan, Christina Tosi, Marguerite Mariscal) gave him operational grounding

## The Authenticity Question

A central theme in NPR's Eat a Peach coverage: Chang's discomfort with being expected to be "authentic" or to speak for Korean-American cuisine. He has said: "I've never said my food's Korean... I've always said that it's American food."

The burden of representation — being a high-profile Asian-American in American food culture — shaped both his creative choices and his mental health struggles.

## Publication Context

Published during the COVID-19 pandemic in September 2020, the book landed as Chang was simultaneously:
- Closing restaurants (Nishi, CCDC had closed)
- Pivoting toward CPG
- Navigating the collapse of the restaurant industry
- Processing new fatherhood (son Hugo born 2018)

The timing made the memoir's themes of failure, rebuilding, and finding meaning beyond restaurants particularly resonant.
